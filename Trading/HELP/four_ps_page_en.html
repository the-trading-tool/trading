4PS Method four_ps_page.py · four_ps.py · indicator/fps.py

The four phase sequence looks for stocks that have already proven they can run harder than their index, and enters at the start of their next trend leg. The idea: the benchmark index historically bounces off its 200-week moving average and then runs 60–90 %. A name that is supposed to outperform must have done considerably more in the past — and tends to do it again.

The four phases

PhaseMeaningHow the app checks it
1 – historyProven trend history Monthly zigzag (default 25 % reversal): at least one completed up-leg above the threshold (default 90 %)
2 – baseSideways consolidation near the highs Longest weekly window (min. 8 weeks) whose high/low range stays within the base width (default 25 %) and whose high sits close to the record high (default ≤ 15 % below, record window 10 years)
3 – breakoutStart of the new trend, entry Close above the base high (+ buffer, default 0.5 %) and above the rising 40-week SMA; stop = max(base low, breakout − 12 %); target = entry + 80 %
4 – trendTrend confirmed, position held Price holds above the breakout level for a few weeks (default 2) and above the 40-week SMA

Exit: close below the 40-week SMA (trend break) or below the stop. Optional trailing stop and profit taking at the target (both off by default — the method typically holds for 12–18 months).

Causal, therefore backtestable: base levels come exclusively from completed weeks, the Phase 1 history from confirmed monthly legs (a high only counts once the counter-move has confirmed it). No value on day D uses data after D — the signals are usable in a backtest and are not hindsight.

The page

  • Index regime (header): benchmark vs its 200-week SMA, size of the running up-leg and a table of past legs.
  • 🔎 Screener: every member of the selected indices, ranked by phase and relative strength. Filters for phase, RS and distance to the breakout. The result can be stored as monitored assets or copied as a ticker list; clicking a row fills the detail tab.
  • 📈 Detail: weekly chart with base, breakout, stop, target and signals, plus a monthly chart of all past up-legs (green = Phase 1 proof), together with the trade plan (stop, target, R/R).
  • 📘 Method: short description and a ready-made strategy block for Multi Strategies.

Sector comparison

Screener and detail view also show where a name stands inside its sector, over 52 weeks:

  • Sector — from asset_info.sector.
  • vs. sector (pp) — own 52-week return minus the sector median. Positive = the name outperforms its sector.
  • Rank in sector % — percentile among the sector peers (100 = best).
  • The filter “Only names above the sector median” hides the laggards.

The peer group is the sector inside the selected universe — not a sector ETF and not a global sector index — so the statement is "better than the typical name of that sector in my selection". Sectors with fewer than 5 members stay empty; a median of two names says nothing. The reference is computed once a day and stored in rotation_cache.db (~8 s for 656 tickers).

The sector comparison is a snapshot and deliberately does not become an fps_* column in the simulation DB: it depends on the selected universe and is not a causal time series. For backtest formulas fps_rs (relative strength against the index) remains the right measure.

Use in the chart, Strategy Finder and Multi Strategies

  • Asset Viewer: enable the fps overlay — it draws base, breakout, stop, target and the buy/sell markers.
  • Buy/sell formulas: e.g. buy (fps_buy > 0), stricter (fps_breakout > 0) & (fps_rs > 0) & (fps_best_trend > 150), sell (fps_sell > 0). Or state-based without signal columns: buy (fps_phase >= 3), sell (fps_phase < 3).
  • Ranking per index via 'order_by': 'fps_best_trend' or 'fps_rs'.
Prerequisite for the backtest: the columns have to be written to the simulation databases once: python asset_perf2.py /backfill:fps /force (plus per year /year:2024 or /all). A full init run fills them as well. Without the backfill everything is zero and the formulas produce no signals.

Parameters

Every threshold can be edited in the ⚙ Method parameters expander and is stored per user in config.db (fps_params). The fps indicator exposes the same parameters in the indicator settings dialog; both paths use the same engine (tradinglib/four_ps.py).

Why those two filters matter: without them a stock that has been falling for a year still qualifies — the 52-week high sinks along with the price, so a relief rally inside a downtrend looks like a breakout (example Kimberly-Clark 2026: 26 % below its record high, falling weekly SMA, buy signal anyway). Measured across S&P 500 + DAX/MDAX/SDAX since 2015 the two filters lift the hit rate from 37.2 % to 40.8 % and the profit factor from 1.96 to 2.02, at about a third fewer signals.

What the method delivers

Backtest over the same 656 names since 2015 (signal to signal, no costs): 3,615 trades, 41.2 % winners, mean +6.6 %, profit factor 2.40, average holding period 190 days. That is the classic trend-following profile: many small losses at the stop, carried by a few large winners. Judging it by the median (which is negative) misreads the method — it lives off the right tail.

Exit length is the biggest lever: SMA20 yields PF 1.68, SMA30 2.03, SMA40 (default) 2.32, SMA50 2.46 — the profit factor rises monotonically with the holding period (99 → 209 days), while the annualised figure stays around 12 % per position. Trailing stop and profit taking at the target hurt (PF 1.99 and 1.93) because they cut off exactly that right tail — both are off by default. The SMA40 + 12 % stop default beat SMA30 + 8 % in all three sub-periods (2015–19, 2020–22, 2023–26) and in both regions.

Careful when carrying this into Multi Strategies: these numbers are per position (signal to signal), not a portfolio with a limited number of slots. Longer holds tie up capital — with num_assets the ranking of the variants may differ.

Data quality: breaks in the price series

The method computes across decades — a level shift in the local price series therefore corrupts everything: record high, zigzag legs, 52-week return and hence the phases. The typical case is a split that was only applied from the changeover day on; every single candle stays internally consistent, only the level of the series jumps.

Example Interactive Brokers: on 2023-12-12 the price in yf_IBKR.db drops from 83.44 to 20.94 (factor 0.25 = a 4:1 split never applied backwards). The pre-break high (94.88) stays in the record window for ten years, so Phase 2 demands a base above 80.65 — while the price traded between 21 and 71. The entire 2024/2025 advance produced no signal; only in 2026, back near the old level, did a buy appear. With adjusted data the method would have bought on 2024-01-22 at 22.77 (about +313 % today).

  • The detail view shows a warning as soon as the loaded history contains such a break; in the screener it appears as ⚠ in the Data column.
  • Check a whole universe with python -m tradinglib.data_quality /gaps /index:^SPX (on Windows run it from PowerShell, otherwise MSYS mangles the /flags). The run lists ticker, date, factor, likely cause and the matching cleanup commands.
  • Cleanup = re-download the prices (get_asset_data.py), then recompute the simulation of the affected indices. Only then are the signals correct.

Not to be confused with scan_ohlc_issues: that checks individual rows (close outside [Low, High]) in the simulation DB and by design does not find series breaks.

Limits

  • Only names with enough history are scored (default 8 years) — recent IPOs drop out.
  • The method is long only and trend following; sideways or bear markets produce failed breakouts. The index regime in the header provides that context.
  • Prices come from the local yf_<TICKER>.db files; splits or pence quotations (LSE) distort the history until they are cleaned up. In a backtest those data errors show up as apparent “trades” of −30 % to −90 % (16 out of 4,209) — check with tradinglib/data_quality.py.
  • A scan across several indices takes about a minute the first time; the result is cached per day (rotation_cache.db).
